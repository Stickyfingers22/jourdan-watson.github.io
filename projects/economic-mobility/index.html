Economic Mobility
in Black America

What actually predicts income and wealth for Black American households, and which gaps close with education versus which don't? A weighted analysis of the Federal Reserve's 2022 Survey of Consumer Finances, set against Opportunity Atlas data on where children grow up.

Core Question
What closes the gap, and what doesn't?
30-Second Executive Summary
The typical Black household has $49,590 in net worth versus $272,000 for the typical White household, a 5.5x gap. Education raises income sharply, about 16% per additional year of schooling. But a degree closes the income gap far more than the wealth gap: Black households with a bachelor's degree earn more than White households with only a high school diploma, yet hold less wealth. And after controlling for education, age, marriage, homeownership and employment, Black household income is still about 20% lower.

I wanted to move past headlines on the racial wealth gap and measure which factors actually move income and wealth for Black households. I used the Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances, the most detailed public data on household wealth in the U.S., and compared Black and White households with population weights.

Three findings stood out. Education is the biggest individual lever on income. But it closes the income gap much more than the wealth gap, because wealth also depends on inheritance and homeownership. And homeownership is where wealth lives: Black homeowners have a median net worth of about $197,000, versus $4,500 for Black renters. I also found that one claim I expected to confirm, that Black graduates get a smaller payoff from a degree, wasn't supported by this data.

The SCF stores each household as five implicates from multiple imputation, so I ran weighted least squares on each implicate and combined estimates with Rubin's rules, which inflates standard errors to reflect imputation uncertainty. The outcome is log household income for heads aged 25–64, with years of schooling, a centered age quadratic, sex, marital status, homeownership, employment, a Black indicator and a Black × bachelor's interaction, using HC3 robust errors.

R² averages 0.50. The Black coefficient is −0.23 (about −20%, p < 0.001); the interaction is −0.01 with a standard error of 0.10, so the data can't distinguish the return to a degree by race. Married is large (+93%) but mostly mechanical: SCF income is household income, so marriage adds a second earner. Homeownership is likely endogenous to income, so I read it as a marker, not a cause. A random forest with permutation importance on the Black subsample agrees on the top drivers. The main limitations are 710 Black households, with only 170 holding a bachelor's degree, and no direct inheritance or parental-income controls in my extract.

CREATE TABLE scf_households (
    household_id   INT,
    implicate      SMALLINT,           -- 1-5, multiple imputation copies
    weight         DECIMAL(12,4),       -- population weight (WGT)
    race           VARCHAR(20),
    age            SMALLINT,
    edu_group      VARCHAR(30),
    educ_years     DECIMAL(4,1),
    married        BOOLEAN,
    homeowner      BOOLEAN,
    working        BOOLEAN,
    income         NUMERIC,
    net_worth      NUMERIC,
    PRIMARY KEY (household_id, implicate)
);

CREATE TABLE atlas_cz (
    cz_id          VARCHAR(10) PRIMARY KEY,
    cz_name        VARCHAR(80),
    hh_income_35   NUMERIC            -- pooled race, all parent incomes
);

SQLQuery 1 — Weighted rates by race and education (CASE + weighted aggregates)
SELECT
    race,
    edu_group,
    COUNT(DISTINCT household_id)                                      AS households,
    ROUND(SUM(weight * income) / SUM(weight), 0)                     AS wtd_mean_income,
    ROUND(100.0 * SUM(CASE WHEN homeowner THEN weight ELSE 0 END)
          / SUM(weight), 1)                                           AS homeownership_pct,
    ROUND(100.0 * SUM(CASE WHEN net_worth <= 0 THEN weight ELSE 0 END)
          / SUM(weight), 1)                                           AS zero_or_negative_wealth_pct
FROM scf_households
WHERE race IN ('Black', 'White')
GROUP BY race, edu_group
ORDER BY race, wtd_mean_income;
WhatPopulation-weighted averages and rates for each race × education cell.
WhySUM(w·x)/SUM(w) is the weighted mean; CASE inside it gives weighted shares.
Business valueShows where gaps are largest so programs can target them.
Output8 rows (2 races × 4 education levels).
Talking pointMedians were computed in Python: weighted medians aren't native SQL.
SQLQuery 2 — The degree paradox: income vs wealth gap side by side (CTE + pivot + RANK)
WITH cells AS (
    SELECT race, edu_group,
           SUM(weight * income)    / SUM(weight) AS income,
           SUM(weight * net_worth) / SUM(weight) AS wealth
    FROM scf_households
    WHERE race IN ('Black', 'White')
    GROUP BY race, edu_group
),
pivoted AS (
    SELECT edu_group,
           MAX(CASE WHEN race = 'Black' THEN income END) AS black_income,
           MAX(CASE WHEN race = 'White' THEN income END) AS white_income,
           MAX(CASE WHEN race = 'Black' THEN wealth END) AS black_wealth,
           MAX(CASE WHEN race = 'White' THEN wealth END) AS white_wealth
    FROM cells GROUP BY edu_group
)
SELECT edu_group,
       ROUND(black_income / white_income, 2)                   AS income_ratio,
       ROUND(black_wealth / white_wealth, 2)                   AS wealth_ratio,
       RANK() OVER (ORDER BY black_wealth / white_wealth)        AS widest_wealth_gap_rank
FROM pivoted
ORDER BY widest_wealth_gap_rank;
WhatPivots races into columns and compares income and wealth ratios per education level.
WhyMAX(CASE…) is a portable pivot that works in any SQL dialect.
Business valueShows education narrows income gaps more than wealth gaps.
OutputIncome ratios closer to 1 than wealth ratios at every level.
Talking pointRanking a derived ratio with a window function, after aggregation.
SQLQuery 3 — Where children grow up: Atlas quintiles (NTILE + window average)
SELECT
    cz_name,
    hh_income_35,
    NTILE(5) OVER (ORDER BY hh_income_35)                       AS quintile,
    ROUND(hh_income_35 - AVG(hh_income_35) OVER (), 0)          AS vs_national_avg,
    RANK() OVER (ORDER BY hh_income_35 DESC)                   AS rank_best
FROM atlas_cz
ORDER BY hh_income_35 DESC;
WhatBuckets all 722 commuting zones into quintiles and compares each to the mean.
WhyAVG() OVER () puts the national mean on every row without a self-join.
Business valueIdentifies the places where adult outcomes lag most.
OutputTop: Dickinson, ND $68,165. Bottom: Mission, TX $27,350.
Talking pointA 2.5x spread from geography alone, before any individual choices.

Section 03 · Statistical Modeling

Weighted regression with
multiple imputation

The model sample is Black and White household heads aged 25–64 with positive income: 2,244 households. The code below is excerpted from analyze_scf.py, which produces every number in this section.

Python1 · Weighted medians across implicates
def wmedian(values, weights):
    d = pd.DataFrame({"v": values, "w": weights}).dropna().sort_values("v")
    c = d["w"].cumsum()
    return float(d.loc[c >= d["w"].sum() / 2, "v"].iloc[0])

# all 5 implicates stacked, weighted by WGT (Federal Reserve convention)
for (race, edu), g in bw.groupby(["race", "edu_group"]):
    print(race, edu, wmedian(g["INCOME"], g["WGT"]), wmedian(g["NETWORTH"], g["WGT"]))
Line by lineSort values, accumulate weights, return the first value past half the total weight.
BusinessMedians resist billionaire outliers that wreck wealth averages.
StatisticsWeights make 4,595 households represent ~131M U.S. households.
Outpute.g. Black, Bachelor's+: income $72,421, net worth $128,150.

Python2 · WLS per implicate, combined with Rubin's rules
X_COLS = ["educ_years", "age_c", "age_c2", "female", "married",
          "homeowner", "working", "black", "black_x_bachelors"]

models = []
for i, g in reg.groupby("implicate"):                 # 5 separate fits
    X = sm.add_constant(g[X_COLS])
    models.append(sm.WLS(g["log_income"], X, weights=g["WGT"]).fit(cov_type="HC3"))

def rubin(models, names):
    est = np.array([m.params[names] for m in models])
    var = np.array([m.bse[names] ** 2 for m in models])
    within  = var.mean(0)                            # average sampling variance
    between = est.var(0, ddof=1)                    # disagreement across implicates
    se = np.sqrt(within + (1 + 1 / len(models)) * between)
    return est.mean(0), se
Line by lineFit the same weighted model on each implicate, then pool coefficients and variances.
BusinessConfidence intervals you can defend in front of skeptical stakeholders.
StatisticsTotal variance = within + (1 + 1/m) × between implicate variance.
OutputBlack: −0.229 (SE 0.061). Educ years: 0.152 (SE 0.008). R² ≈ 0.50.
Interview"Treating 22,975 rows as independent would understate SEs by ~√5."

Why these methods, and where they break

Weighted OLS

Why
Interpretable coefficients: "each year of school is associated with 16% higher income."
Assumes
Linear in log income; no major omitted variable correlated with the predictors.
Read it as
Associations, not causes. Homeownership likely follows income as much as it drives it.
Limits
No inheritance or parental-income controls in this extract; the Black gap partly reflects them.
So what
A 20% gap remains among otherwise similar households.

Multiple imputation

Why
The SCF imputes missing answers five times; ignoring that understates uncertainty.
Assumes
The Fed's imputation model is reasonable (missing at random given covariates).
Read it as
Wider, more honest confidence intervals.
Limits
Rubin's rules don't fix model misspecification.
So what
Results reflect what the data can actually support.

Random forest

Why
Checks the OLS story without assuming linearity or specifying interactions.
Assumes
Little; but small samples make importances noisy.
Read it as
A ranking, not effect sizes. Permutation importance on held-out data avoids bias toward continuous features.
Limits
Correlated features (age, working) split credit and can show near-zero importance.
So what
Marriage and education lead in both models: the finding is robust.

Hypothesis test on the interaction

Why
Tests whether a bachelor's degree pays off differently for Black households.
Assumes
Enough Black graduates to detect a gap (only 170 households).
Read it as
−1.4%, 95% CI about −19% to +20%: not significant.
Limits
Low power. "Not significant" is not "no difference."
So what
I don't claim a degree-return gap this data can't show.

Implications

What follows
from the evidence

01
Pair income growth with asset-building
Education raises income, but income alone isn't closing the wealth gap. Programs that convert income into assets (retirement auto-enrollment, matched savings, first-time investing) target the part of the gap education misses.Follows from: the degree paradox; stock ownership 13% vs 24%
02
Homeownership is the main wealth channel
For most households, home equity is the wealth. Down-payment assistance and fair lending enforcement address the 25-point homeownership gap directly, though causality runs both ways and the regression can't separate them.Follows from: homeowner vs renter net worth; 46.7% vs 72.2% ownership
03
The unexplained gap needs better data, not assumptions
A 20% gap after controls is consistent with discrimination, differences in parental wealth, occupation and location. Pinning down the mix requires linked parental income and employer data, which is the next analytical step.Follows from: Black coefficient −0.23 with omitted-variable caveats

STAR method
answer

S
Situation
The racial wealth gap is widely discussed but often with headline numbers that don't separate what education fixes from what it doesn't. I wanted a quantitative answer from authoritative data.
T
Task
Identify which household factors predict income and wealth for Black Americans, and measure how much of the gap with White households remains once those factors are accounted for.
A

Letting the data overrule my hypothesis was the most important part.

Common questions

Census surveys measure income well but say almost nothing about wealth. The SCF is the Federal Reserve's dedicated wealth survey: it measures assets, debts and net worth in detail and oversamples wealthy households so the top of the distribution is captured. Since my question was about wealth as much as income, the SCF was the right source, at the cost of a smaller sample.
When respondents skip questions, the Fed fills in plausible values five separate times, producing five versions of each household. If you treat all 22,975 rows as independent, you overstate your sample size about fivefold and get standard errors that are far too small. Running the model on each implicate and combining with Rubin's rules adds the between-implicate variance back in, so the confidence intervals reflect the real uncertainty.
No, and I'm careful not to claim that. It means that among households similar on the variables I controlled for, Black household income is about 20% lower. That gap is consistent with discrimination, but also with factors I couldn't control for in this extract: parental wealth and inheritance, occupation, industry and region. The honest framing is that observable differences in education and household structure don't explain it, and identifying the causes needs richer data.
Mostly mechanics. SCF income is household income, so a married household often has two earners. The +93% coefficient largely reflects that second income, not some return to marriage itself. I'd flag this immediately if presenting it, because it's the kind of result that gets misread. Modeling individual earnings or adding a two-earner indicator would separate the effects.
I went in expecting Black graduates to get a smaller income boost from a bachelor's degree, which some research reports. The interaction term came out at −1.4% with a confidence interval from roughly −19% to +20%: not significant. With only 170 Black degree holders, the test has low power, so I report it as inconclusive rather than as evidence of no gap. I removed the claim from the project rather than keep a finding I liked but couldn't support.
