Core Question
What sets the price of a cup, and who captures the value?
30-Second Executive Summary
Roughly two-thirds of the world's coffee comes from five countries, and Brazil alone grows about a third. That concentration means one bad Brazilian harvest moves global prices: arabica futures hit record highs above $4 per pound in early 2025 after drought cut supply. Yet in a café, the beans are a small slice of a $5–6 latte; rent, labor, milk and margin are most of it. The value in coffee is captured downstream, by the brands that control the experience, not the farms that grow the crop.

I built a three-part analysis on coffee because it is a rare product where you can follow one item through a full value chain with public data. Part one uses USDA production data and IMF price series to show how concentrated supply is and how Brazilian harvests drive price shocks. Part two looks at the café industry: Starbucks store growth from its 10-K filings and a cost breakdown of a latte to show where the money goes. Part three covers consumption: caffeine content by drink against the FDA's 400 mg guideline, with an interactive calculator.

Technically, it covers SQL with CTEs and window functions for year-over-year changes and rankings, a seasonal time series decomposition and Holt-Winters forecast of prices, K-means clustering of countries into producer and consumer profiles, and a regression of price changes on lagged Brazilian output.

The price model is the interesting part. Monthly arabica prices are non-stationary with heavy volatility clustering, so I log-transform, check an ADF test, and work in log returns for the regression. The explanatory variable is the year-over-year change in Brazilian production lagged one crop year, because futures traders price expected supply, and the realized harvest confirms or corrects that. I report the coefficient with HAC (Newey-West) standard errors, since monthly residuals are autocorrelated.

For the forecast I use additive Holt-Winters on the log series and evaluate on a held-out final 24 months against a naive random-walk benchmark using MAPE. Commodity prices are close to a random walk, so the honest result is usually that the model barely beats naive; I treat the forecast as a scenario band, not a point prediction. The clustering standardizes production, consumption per capita and export share, picks k with silhouette scores, and the result separates large exporters, high-consumption importers, and producers that also drink most of what they grow, like Ethiopia.

The main limitation is that the price series is a global average; it hides quality premiums and the share of the export price that reaches farmers, which needs farm-gate data I don't have.

The Global Economy

From the farm:
who grows it, and what it costs

Coffee grows in a narrow band between the tropics, mostly on smallholder farms. Supply is concentrated, demand is spread across the world, and the price in between is set on futures exchanges in New York (arabica) and London (robusta).

SQL · Schema and queries

A small star schema: a country dimension with yearly production and consumption facts, plus a monthly price fact table.

SQLSchema — coffee_warehouse
CREATE TABLE dim_country (
    country_code   CHAR(3) PRIMARY KEY,   -- ISO-3
    country_name   VARCHAR(80),
    region         VARCHAR(40),
    population_m   DECIMAL(8,2)
);

CREATE TABLE fact_supply (               -- one row per country per marketing year
    country_code   CHAR(3) REFERENCES dim_country,
    market_year    SMALLINT,
    production_kb  INT,       -- thousand 60 kg bags
    arabica_kb     INT,
    robusta_kb     INT,
    exports_kb     INT,
    consumption_kb INT,
    PRIMARY KEY (country_code, market_year)
);

CREATE TABLE fact_price (                -- monthly benchmark prices
    price_month    DATE PRIMARY KEY,
    arabica_c_lb   DECIMAL(7,2),  -- US cents per pound
    robusta_c_lb   DECIMAL(7,2)
);
WhatTwo fact tables at different grains (yearly supply, monthly price) sharing a country dimension.
WhyKeeping grains separate avoids duplicating yearly values onto 12 monthly rows.
Business valueLets an importer join supply shocks to price moves without manual spreadsheets.
Output~60 countries × 15 years of supply; ~190 monthly price rows.
Talking pointExplain grain choice: mixing grains in one table is a classic double-counting bug.
SQLQuery 1 — Market share, rank and running total (CTE + window functions)
WITH y AS (
    SELECT c.country_name, s.production_kb
    FROM fact_supply s
    JOIN dim_country c USING (country_code)
    WHERE s.market_year = 2024
)
SELECT
    country_name,
    production_kb,
    RANK() OVER (ORDER BY production_kb DESC)                 AS producer_rank,
    ROUND(100.0 * production_kb / SUM(production_kb) OVER (), 1)   AS share_pct,
    ROUND(100.0 * SUM(production_kb) OVER (ORDER BY production_kb DESC)
          / SUM(production_kb) OVER (), 1)                         AS cumulative_pct
FROM y
ORDER BY producer_rank
LIMIT 10;
WhatRanks producers and computes each country's share and the running cumulative share.
WhyA running total answers "how many countries make up most of supply?" in one pass.
Business valueQuantifies supply concentration risk for a buyer sourcing beans.
OutputBrazil #1 near 37%; cumulative share passes ~65% by the fifth country.
Talking pointSUM() OVER () with an empty window is the grand total; adding ORDER BY makes it running.
SQLQuery 2 — Year-over-year price change and shock flag (LAG + CASE)
WITH annual AS (
    SELECT EXTRACT(YEAR FROM price_month) AS yr,
           AVG(arabica_c_lb)               AS avg_price
    FROM fact_price
    GROUP BY 1
),
brazil AS (
    SELECT market_year AS yr, production_kb
    FROM fact_supply WHERE country_code = 'BRA'
)
SELECT
    a.yr,
    ROUND(a.avg_price, 1)                                              AS avg_price,
    ROUND(100.0 * (a.avg_price / LAG(a.avg_price) OVER (ORDER BY a.yr) - 1), 1) AS price_yoy_pct,
    ROUND(100.0 * (b.production_kb / LAG(b.production_kb) OVER (ORDER BY a.yr) - 1), 1) AS brazil_yoy_pct,
    CASE
        WHEN a.avg_price / LAG(a.avg_price) OVER (ORDER BY a.yr) > 1.25 THEN 'price shock'
        WHEN a.avg_price / LAG(a.avg_price) OVER (ORDER BY a.yr) < 0.80 THEN 'price collapse'
        ELSE 'normal'
    END                                                              AS regime
FROM annual a
LEFT JOIN brazil b USING (yr)
ORDER BY a.yr;
WhatAnnual average price, its year-over-year change, Brazil's output change, and a labeled regime.
WhyPuts supply and price side by side so you can see whether shocks line up with harvests.
Business valueA roaster can flag years where hedging would have mattered most.
Output2021, 2022 and 2024 flagged as shocks; 2018–19 as a soft period.
Talking pointLEFT JOIN keeps price years even when supply data lags a year behind.

The Coffee Shop Industry

To the counter:
how a commodity becomes a brand

A pound of green arabica makes roughly 40 to 50 cups. Even at record prices, the beans in one latte cost well under a dollar. The rest of the menu price pays for the location, the people, the milk, and the brand.

SQLQuery 3 — Store growth by region with ranking (JOIN + PARTITION BY)
-- fact_stores: one row per fiscal year per region, from 10-K store tables
WITH growth AS (
    SELECT
        region,
        fiscal_year,
        store_count,
        store_count - LAG(store_count) OVER (
            PARTITION BY region ORDER BY fiscal_year)   AS net_new
    FROM fact_stores
)
SELECT
    region,
    SUM(net_new)                                     AS net_new_2019_2024,
    DENSE_RANK() OVER (ORDER BY SUM(net_new) DESC)      AS growth_rank,
    ROUND(100.0 * SUM(net_new) / SUM(SUM(net_new)) OVER (), 1) AS share_of_growth_pct
FROM growth
WHERE fiscal_year BETWEEN 2020 AND 2024
GROUP BY region
ORDER BY growth_rank;
WhatNet new stores per region over five years and each region's share of total growth.
WhyPARTITION BY restarts LAG for each region so years don't bleed across regions.
Business valueShows where a chain is betting its expansion capital.
OutputChina and U.S. account for most net new stores.
Talking pointSUM(SUM(x)) OVER (): an aggregate inside a window, evaluated after GROUP BY.

The Habit

Into the bloodstream:
how much caffeine is too much?

Caffeine is the most widely used psychoactive substance in the world. Regular users build tolerance and get withdrawal headaches when they stop, which is part of why coffee demand barely moves when prices rise. The FDA puts the safe daily amount for most healthy adults at about 400 mg.

Python1 · Load the warehouse tables produced by fetch_data.py
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

supply = pd.read_csv("data/supply.csv")              # country, market_year, production_kb, ...
price  = pd.read_csv("data/price_monthly.csv",       # date, arabica_c_lb
                     parse_dates=["date"], index_col="date")
price  = price.asfreq("MS")                           # enforce month-start frequency
price["log_p"] = np.log(price["arabica_c_lb"])          # multiplicative swings become additive
print(price.tail(), supply.shape)
Line by lineReads two tables, sets a monthly index, adds a log price column.
BusinessOne reproducible source of truth instead of copied spreadsheet numbers.
StatisticsLog prices make a 20% move look the same at 100¢ and at 300¢.
OutputLast five monthly prices and the supply table's dimensions.
Interview"I log-transform because commodity moves are proportional, not absolute."
Python2 · Decompose and forecast price (Statsmodels)
from statsmodels.tsa.seasonal import STL
from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import adfuller
from statsmodels.tsa.holtwinters import ExponentialSmoothing

y = price["log_p"].dropna()

# Stationarity check: a high p-value means the level wanders (random-walk-like)
print("ADF p-value:", round(adfuller(y)[1], 3))

# Split trend / seasonal / remainder
stl = STL(y, period=12, robust=True).fit()

# Hold out the last 24 months for honest evaluation
train, test = y[:-24], y[-24:]
hw = ExponentialSmoothing(train, trend="add", damped_trend=True,
                          seasonal="add", seasonal_periods=12).fit()
fc = np.exp(hw.forecast(24))
naive = np.repeat(np.exp(train.iloc[-1]), 24)       # random-walk benchmark

mape = lambda a, f: np.mean(np.abs((a - f) / a)) * 100
actual = np.exp(test)
print(f"Holt-Winters MAPE: {mape(actual, fc):.1f}%  |  Naive MAPE: {mape(actual, naive):.1f}%")
Line by lineADF test, STL decomposition, damped Holt-Winters fit on training months, compared to naive.
BusinessGives a buyer a price range for budgeting a year of bean purchases.
StatisticsDamped trend stops the forecast from extrapolating a spike forever.
OutputTwo MAPE values; the gap between them is the model's real value.
Interview"Always beat naive first; commodity prices are hard to forecast."
Python3 · Does Brazil's harvest move the price? (OLS with Newey-West errors)
import statsmodels.api as sm

annual = price["arabica_c_lb"].resample("YS").mean().to_frame("p")
annual["yr"] = annual.index.year
bra = supply.query("country_code == 'BRA'")[["market_year", "production_kb"]]

df = annual.merge(bra, left_on="yr", right_on="market_year")
df["price_ret"]  = np.log(df["p"]).diff()                  # % change in price
df["brazil_chg"] = np.log(df["production_kb"]).diff()       # % change in output
df = df.dropna()

X = sm.add_constant(df[["brazil_chg"]])
model = sm.OLS(df["price_ret"], X).fit(cov_type="HAC", cov_kwds={"maxlags": 1})
print(model.summary().tables[1])
# Expect a negative coefficient: bigger Brazilian crop → lower price
Line by lineAnnual prices joined to Brazil output, both converted to log changes, regressed.
BusinessTells a buyer how much to worry when Brazil forecasts a short crop.
StatisticsDifferencing removes trends that would create a fake correlation; HAC fixes autocorrelated errors.
OutputCoefficient, robust standard error, p-value for brazil_chg.
Interview"With ~15 annual points, I report direction and uncertainty, not a precise elasticity."
Python4 · Cluster countries into coffee profiles (K-means) + ANOVA
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score
from scipy.stats import f_oneway

latest = supply.query("market_year == market_year.max()").copy()
latest["export_ratio"]  = latest.exports_kb / latest.production_kb.clip(lower=1)
latest["log_production"] = np.log1p(latest.production_kb)
latest["log_consumption"] = np.log1p(latest.consumption_kb)
feats = ["log_production", "log_consumption", "export_ratio"]

Z = StandardScaler().fit_transform(latest[feats])     # equal weight per feature
scores = {k: silhouette_score(Z, KMeans(k, n_init=20, random_state=42).fit_predict(Z))
          for k in range(2, 7)}
best_k = max(scores, key=scores.get)
latest["cluster"] = KMeans(best_k, n_init=20, random_state=42).fit_predict(Z)
print("silhouette by k:", scores)
print(latest.groupby("cluster")[feats].mean().round(2))

# Do the clusters differ on export_ratio? One-way ANOVA
groups = [g.export_ratio.values for _, g in latest.groupby("cluster")]
print("ANOVA:", f_oneway(*groups))
Line by lineBuilds three features, standardizes, chooses k by silhouette, labels clusters, tests differences.
BusinessSegments markets: where to source beans vs where to sell finished coffee.
StatisticsLogs tame Brazil's size; scaling stops one feature from dominating distances.
OutputSilhouette per k, cluster means, an F-statistic and p-value.
Interview"ANOVA here is descriptive; clusters are built to differ, so a low p-value is expected."

Why each method, and where it breaks

Time series forecast

Why
Monthly prices have trend and mild seasonality around harvest cycles.
Assumes
Past structure continues; errors are roughly constant in log terms.
Read it as
A scenario range for budgeting, not a call on next month's price.
Limits
Cannot anticipate frosts, droughts or policy shocks.
So what
Pair with hedging rather than relying on the point forecast.

Regression

Why
Gives a direction and size for the Brazil–price link.
Assumes
Linear relation in log changes; no big omitted driver correlated with Brazil.
Read it as
A 10% bigger crop is associated with a coefficient × 10% price move.
Limits
Small sample; Vietnam, currency (BRL/USD) and demand also matter.
So what
Watch Brazil's crop forecasts as an early warning signal.

Clustering

Why
No labels exist for "type of coffee country"; K-means finds natural groups.
Assumes
Roughly round, similar-sized clusters in scaled space.
Read it as
Profiles, not hard categories; check border countries by hand.
Limits
Sensitive to feature choice and scaling.
So what
Guides sourcing and market-entry shortlists.

ANOVA & validation

Why
Confirms cluster differences and compares forecast error on held-out data.
Assumes
ANOVA: independent groups, similar variances.
Read it as
Out-of-sample MAPE vs naive is the number that matters.
Limits
In-sample fit always looks better than reality.
So what
Only report models that beat a simple benchmark.

Recommendations

If you ran a
coffee business

01
Hedge against Brazil, not against "coffee"
Supply risk is concentrated in one country's weather. A roaster should tie hedging and inventory decisions to Brazilian crop forecasts (CONAB, USDA) and diversify sourcing toward Colombia, Central America and East Africa for arabica.
02
Price on experience, not on beans
Because beans are a small share of cost, cafés can absorb bean spikes for a while. Labor scheduling and occupancy are the bigger levers on margin; menu increases should be timed to sustained price levels, not short spikes.
03
Market caffeine clarity
Many drinkers do not know a large cold brew can carry 200–300 mg. Listing caffeine per size and offering half-caf options serves health-aware customers and the older, daily-drinking segment without reducing visits.
